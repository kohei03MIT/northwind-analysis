Where growth comes from, who it depends on, and what discounts buy

Three questions a sales manager can act on, answered from 830 orders and $1.27M of revenue: which countries are growing, how dependent we are on a few customers, and whether discounts win bigger orders or just give revenue away.

Summary: one story in three parts

The company more than doubled revenue between Jan–Apr 1997 and Jan–Apr 1998 (+121%, from $191k to $422k). The three analyses show that this growth, and its risks, run through a handful of key accounts:

  • Country growth follows key accounts. QUICK-Stop supplied about half of Germany's Jan–Apr 1998 revenue, and Ernst Handel accounts for 82% of all Austrian revenue. Our "growing markets" are partly a few large customers.
  • One lost account explains Canada's decline. Mère Paillarde, a top-10 customer, placed its last order on 30 Oct 1997. Canada fell 24%, but without Mère Paillarde the rest of Canada grew from $4.8k to $11.5k (+141%), in line with the company. Nobody flagged the lapse.
  • Deep discounts buy no extra volume, and a few key accounts get more than their share. Discounted lines carry about 26% more units than full-price lines of the same product, but that lift is about the same at 5% off as at 25% off. The top 10 customers receive a slightly larger share of discounts than of revenue (51% vs 45%), driven mainly by Save-a-lot Markets, Hungry Owl and Ernst Handel; others in the top 10, such as Rattlesnake Canyon, get well below their share. Discounts do not look like a tool that wins extra sales.

Question 8

Which countries are growing, and which are flat or shrinking?

Why it matters

Sales and marketing budgets should follow growth. Knowing which markets are expanding, and which are stalling while the rest of the company grows, tells the manager where to invest and where to investigate.

Approach

  • Revenue per order = Σ UnitPrice × Quantity × (1 − Discount), with the country taken from the customer's record.
  • Monthly revenue per country from Jul 1996 to Apr 1998, with a 3-month rolling average to smooth out single large orders.
  • Growth compares Jan–Apr 1997 with Jan–Apr 1998, the same months a year apart, because 1996 and 1998 are incomplete years.
  • The 8 countries with fewer than 20 orders in total are pooled as "Other".

Main finding

Growth is concentrated in the USA, Germany and Brazil, which together added $132k of the company's $231k increase. France is flat (+5%) and Canada is shrinking (−24%) while the company more than doubled. Canada's decline is entirely the loss of one customer, Mère Paillarde (see question 2).

Business implication

  • Put new sales capacity behind the USA, Germany and Brazil, where demand is already accelerating.
  • Treat France as the priority to diagnose: a mid-sized market (6% of revenue) that stalled while every other large market grew.
  • Treat Canada as a win-back, not a market problem: re-engage Mère Paillarde, since the rest of Canada is growing.

Question 2

How much of our revenue depends on a few customers?

Why it matters

If a small number of customers carry a large share of revenue, losing even one is a material risk. Concentration decides whether key-account managers and early-warning tracking are worth their cost.

Approach

  • Total revenue per customer over all orders, ranked from largest to smallest.
  • Each customer's share of revenue, the top 1/3/5/10 shares, and how many customers it takes to reach 50% and 80% of revenue.
  • To test whether concentration is changing, compare periods of equal length (Jan–Apr 1997 vs Jan–Apr 1998). A shorter period always looks more concentrated, because fewer customers order in it.

Main finding

Three key accounts, QUICK-Stop, Ernst Handel and Save-a-lot Markets, bring in about 8% of revenue each and a quarter of revenue together; every other customer is under 4%. The top 10 bring in 45%. Concentration is stable: the company doubled without becoming more dependent on its largest customers. But one top-10 customer, Mère Paillarde (Canada), stopped ordering after 30 Oct 1997 and went unnoticed.

Business implication

  • Assign a named key-account manager to each of the top 3 customers; together they are a quarter of the business.
  • Add a simple early-warning rule: flag any top-20 customer with no order in 90 days. It would have flagged Mère Paillarde by late January 1998.
  • Concentration itself is not worsening, so the priority is protecting existing key accounts rather than urgently diversifying.

Question 4

Do discounts increase order size, or just give away revenue?

Revenue given away, not profit. The dataset has no cost data, so this section measures revenue given away and revenue gained. A discount that breaks even on revenue still loses margin.

Why it matters

Discounts are routine: 38.9% of order lines carry one, and together they gave away $88.7k, or 7.0% of net revenue. If discounts win larger orders, they pay for themselves in revenue; if not, they are a direct giveaway. The answer decides how much discount authority the sales team should have.

Approach

  • Compare each discounted order line with full-price lines of the same product at the same unit price. Every product changed list price during the period, so this keeps price changes and product mix out of the comparison. 829 of 838 discounted lines have such a match.
  • Measure the extra quantity on discounted lines, and compare it with the extra quantity needed to break even on revenue: a 25% discount needs a third more units just to bring in what a full-price line does.
  • Repeat for each discount level (5, 10, 15, 20, 25%), with 95% intervals from 1,000 bootstrap resamples.

Main finding

Discounted lines carry about 26% more units than full-price lines of the same product, but deeper discounts do not add more: the lift is +24% at 5% off and +32% at 25% off, with heavily overlapping intervals. Meanwhile the cost climbs steeply. 20–25% discounts give away $55k, 62% of all discount dollars, and at 25% off the extra quantity only just covers the discount (+32% against +33% needed). Overall, discounted lines bring in about 7% more revenue than a full-price line, an effect too small to tell apart from zero.

Business implication

  • Cap routine discounts at 5–10%. They come with about the same extra volume as 25%, at a fraction of the cost.
  • Require approval for discounts above 10%, with a stated reason such as a competing offer or a volume commitment.
  • Review discount terms account by account rather than for "key accounts" as a group. The top 10 customers (see question 2) receive a slightly larger share of discounts than of revenue (51% vs 45%; an effective discount of 7.4% against 5.8% for other customers), but only 5 of the 10 get more than their share. The gap comes mainly from Save-a-lot Markets (12.8% of discount dollars vs 8.2% of revenue), Hungry Owl (8.3% vs 3.9%) and Ernst Handel (9.4% vs 8.3%); start the review with them.
  • Add product costs to the data before judging profitability; revenue alone cannot show whether any discount level is profitable.

The data shows that discounts and larger quantities go together, not which causes which. Large orders may simply earn a discount. The flat lift across discount levels fits that explanation: if buyers were responding to price, deeper discounts should bring noticeably more volume.

Data notes

Source
data/Northwind Database.xlsx: 830 orders and 2,155 order lines from 4 Jul 1996 to 6 May 1998. All joins are complete: every order has line items and a known customer.
Revenue
UnitPrice × Quantity × (1 − Discount) from the Orders Details sheet, i.e. the price actually charged. The hand-made price columns in the Product sheet (Price, Cheap, NewPrice, IsCheap) are not used. Total revenue is $1,265,793.
Revenue, not profit
The dataset has no cost data, so every figure is revenue. Margins cannot be measured, and the discount analysis shows revenue given away, not profit lost.
Partial months and years
May 1998 contains only 6 days (14 orders, $18k) and is excluded from monthly trends. July 1996 starts on the 4th and is kept. Because 1996 and 1998 are incomplete, growth compares Jan–Apr 1997 with Jan–Apr 1998, not calendar years.
Countries with few orders
Argentina, Belgium, Denmark, Ireland, Norway, Poland, Portugal and Switzerland each have fewer than 20 orders and are pooled as "Other (8 countries)" in both country charts. A growth rate computed on fewer than 5 orders in Jan–Apr 1997 is marked with a star (for example Spain +2274%, Sweden +1097%).
Country
Taken from the Customers sheet. Four orders were shipped to a different country than the customer's; they count under the customer's country.
Customers
2 of 91 customers (FISSA, PARIS) never ordered and are excluded from shares and ranks.
Discount comparison
Each discounted line is compared with full-price lines of the same product at the same unit price, because every product changed list price during the period. 9 of 838 discounted lines have no such match and are left out of the comparison. Eight lines with odd discounts of 1–6% are counted in the 5% band. Intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals from 1,000 resamples with a fixed seed.
Discounts by customer
Revenue given away = UnitPrice × Quantity × Discount for every discounted line (all 838, $88,666 in total), assigned to customers through the Orders sheet. "Top 10" uses the same revenue ranking as question 2. The effective discount rate is revenue given away divided by revenue before discounts, pooled over each group of customers. Per-customer figures are in data/processed/discount_by_customer.csv.
