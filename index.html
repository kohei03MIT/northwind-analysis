Where growth comes from, who it depends on, and who sells it

Three questions a sales manager can act on, answered from 830 orders and $1.27M of revenue: which countries are growing, how dependent we are on a few customers, and how our employees compare.

Summary: one story in three parts

The company more than doubled revenue between Jan–Apr 1997 and Jan–Apr 1998 (+121%, from $191k to $422k). The three analyses show that this growth, and its risks, run through a handful of key accounts:

  • Country growth follows key accounts. QUICK-Stop supplied about half of Germany's Jan–Apr 1998 revenue, and Ernst Handel accounts for 82% of all Austrian revenue. Our "growing markets" are partly a few large customers.
  • One lost account explains Canada's decline. Mère Paillarde, a top-10 customer, placed its last order on 30 Oct 1997. Canada fell 24%, but without Mère Paillarde the rest of Canada grew from $4.8k to $11.5k (+141%), in line with the company. Nobody flagged the lapse.
  • Employees are the people who keep those accounts. Sales volume differs widely across the six sales reps, while late shipments are rare and spread evenly, so the useful lever is account coverage and coaching, not shipping blame.

Question 8

Which countries are growing, and which are flat or shrinking?

Why it matters

Sales and marketing budgets should follow growth. Knowing which markets are expanding, and which are stalling while the rest of the company grows, tells the manager where to invest and where to investigate.

Approach

  • Revenue per order = Σ UnitPrice × Quantity × (1 − Discount), with the country taken from the customer's record.
  • Monthly revenue per country from Jul 1996 to Apr 1998, with a 3-month rolling average to smooth out single large orders.
  • Growth compares Jan–Apr 1997 with Jan–Apr 1998, the same months a year apart, because 1996 and 1998 are incomplete years.
  • The 8 countries with fewer than 20 orders in total are pooled as "Other".

Main finding

Growth is concentrated in the USA, Germany and Brazil, which together added $132k of the company's $231k increase. France is flat (+5%) and Canada is shrinking (−24%) while the company more than doubled. Canada's decline is entirely the loss of one customer, Mère Paillarde (see question 2).

Business implication

  • Put new sales capacity behind the USA, Germany and Brazil, where demand is already accelerating.
  • Treat France as the priority to diagnose: a mid-sized market (6% of revenue) that stalled while every other large market grew.
  • Treat Canada as a win-back, not a market problem: re-engage Mère Paillarde, since the rest of Canada is growing.

Question 2

How much of our revenue depends on a few customers?

Why it matters

If a small number of customers carry a large share of revenue, losing even one is a material risk. Concentration decides whether key-account managers and early-warning tracking are worth their cost.

Approach

  • Total revenue per customer over all orders, ranked from largest to smallest.
  • Each customer's share of revenue, the top 1/3/5/10 shares, and how many customers it takes to reach 50% and 80% of revenue.
  • To test whether concentration is changing, compare periods of equal length (Jan–Apr 1997 vs Jan–Apr 1998). A shorter period always looks more concentrated, because fewer customers order in it.

Main finding

Three key accounts, QUICK-Stop, Ernst Handel and Save-a-lot Markets, bring in about 8% of revenue each and a quarter of revenue together; every other customer is under 4%. The top 10 bring in 45%. Concentration is stable: the company doubled without becoming more dependent on its largest customers. But one top-10 customer, Mère Paillarde (Canada), stopped ordering after 30 Oct 1997 and went unnoticed.

Business implication

  • Assign a named key-account manager to each of the top 3 customers; together they are a quarter of the business.
  • Add a simple early-warning rule: flag any top-20 customer with no order in 90 days. It would have flagged Mère Paillarde by late January 1998.
  • Concentration itself is not worsening, so the priority is protecting existing key accounts rather than urgently diversifying.

Question 6

How do employees compare on sales and on fulfillment quality?

Why it matters

Incentives, coaching and territory decisions need to rest on differences that are real. Looking at revenue together with order count, order size and late shipments shows how each person sells, and whether any difference in delivery quality is large enough to act on.

Approach

  • Per employee: revenue, order count, average order value (revenue ÷ orders), and late-shipment rate.
  • An order is late when its ShippedDate is after its RequiredDate. The 21 orders not yet shipped are left out of the late rate, because their outcome is unknown.
  • Only 37 orders were late in total, so each late rate is shown with a 95% confidence interval and its raw count.

The fair comparison is the six Sales Representatives. Andrew Fuller (Vice President, Sales), Steven Buchanan (Sales Manager) and Laura Callahan (Inside Sales Coordinator) have different jobs, and their lower order counts may reflect that, so they are shown in the chart but left out of the comparison below.

Main finding

Among the six reps, revenue ranges 3.2×, from $233k (Peacock) to $74k (Suyama), and the reps reach it in different ways. Peacock leads on order count; King and Dodsworth win large orders (about $1,750) but fewer of them; Suyama handles many small orders ($1,103). Late shipments do not separate anyone: every employee's 95% interval includes the 4.6% company rate, and the highest rate (Dodsworth, 9.5%) rests on just 4 late orders.

Business implication

  • Coach to each rep's gap: more orders for King and Dodsworth, larger orders for Suyama.
  • Do not reward or penalize individuals on late-shipment rate; the differences are within noise.
  • Fix lateness as a process issue instead. Orders due 14 days after ordering ship late 17.6% of the time, against 3.7% for 28-day orders. That is a warehouse and carrier question, not an individual one.

Data notes

Source
data/Northwind Database.xlsx: 830 orders and 2,155 order lines from 4 Jul 1996 to 6 May 1998. All joins are complete: every order has line items, a known customer and a known employee.
Revenue
UnitPrice × Quantity × (1 − Discount) from the Orders Details sheet, i.e. the price actually charged. The hand-made price columns in the Product sheet (Price, Cheap, NewPrice, IsCheap) are not used. Total revenue is $1,265,793.
Revenue, not profit
The dataset has no cost data, so every figure is revenue. Margins cannot be measured.
Partial months and years
May 1998 contains only 6 days (14 orders, $18k) and is excluded from monthly trends. July 1996 starts on the 4th and is kept. Because 1996 and 1998 are incomplete, growth compares Jan–Apr 1997 with Jan–Apr 1998, not calendar years.
Countries with few orders
Argentina, Belgium, Denmark, Ireland, Norway, Poland, Portugal and Switzerland each have fewer than 20 orders and are pooled as "Other (8 countries)" in both country charts. A growth rate computed on fewer than 5 orders in Jan–Apr 1997 is marked with a star (for example Spain +2274%, Sweden +1097%).
Country
Taken from the Customers sheet. Four orders were shipped to a different country than the customer's; they count under the customer's country.
Customers
2 of 91 customers (FISSA, PARIS) never ordered and are excluded from shares and ranks.
Late shipments
Late = ShippedDate after RequiredDate. 21 orders, all placed on or after 8 Apr 1998, had not shipped by the end of the data, and none was past its required date; they are left out of the late rate. Intervals are 95% Wilson intervals.
